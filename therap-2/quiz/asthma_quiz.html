Scenario 1 Management of Acute Severe Asthma (Status Asthmaticus)

A 22-year-old university student is brought to the Emergency Department with extreme breathlessness, severe chest tightness, and audible wheezing. She can only speak in broken, single words. On examination: Respiratory Rate = 34 breaths/min, Heart Rate = 128 beats/min, SpO2 = 88% on room air, and Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) is 38% of predicted value. Auscultation reveals widespread bilateral inspiratory and expiratory polyphonic wheezes with poor air entry.
1.1 Classify the severity of this patient's acute asthma presentation based on clinical criteria and state the immediate first-line pharmacological interventions required within the first hour.
1.2 Describe the cellular mechanism of action of inhaled short-acting β2-agonists (SABA) and explain why coadministration of ipratropium bromide provides synergistic bronchodilation.
1.3 Discuss the role, rationale, and mechanism of systemic corticosteroids (e.g., IV hydrocortisone or oral prednisolone) in this acute setting, including why they are not effective as immediate acute relievers.
1.4 If the patient demonstrates a poor response after 1 hour of continuous nebulization and remains severely hypoxemic, what second-line adjunctive intravenous agent should be administered, and what is its mechanism of action?
1.5 Explain the potential systemic metabolic and cardiac adverse effects that may arise from high-dose, repeated administration of nebulized salbutamol during this emergency protocol.

Model Answers

1.1 Classified as Severe Acute Asthma / Life-Threatening Exacerbation (PEF < 50%, RR > 25, HR > 120, SpO2 < 92%, inability to complete sentences). Immediate interventions: Oxygen to achieve SpO2 94–98%, high-dose nebulized SABA (Salbutamol 2.5–5 mg), nebulized Muscarinic Antagonist (Ipratropium bromide 0.5 mg), and early administration of systemic corticosteroids (Oral Prednisolone 40–50 mg or IV Hydrocortisone 100–200 mg).
1.2 SABA binds β2-adrenoceptors → activates Gs protein → stimulates adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP → activates Protein Kinase A (PKA) → ↓ intracellular Ca2+ → bronchial smooth muscle relaxation. Synergy occurs because SABA directly stimulates adrenergic bronchodilation while Ipratropium blocks parasympathetic cholinergic bronchoconstriction (vagal tone), addressing two distinct physiological pathways.
1.3 Corticosteroids reduce airway mucosal edema, decrease inflammatory cytokine production, inhibit leukotriene synthesis via lipocortin induction, and up-regulate β2-adrenoceptor expression. They are not fast-acting relievers because their mechanism depends on gene transcription/protein synthesis, requiring 4 to 6 hours for clinical onset.
1.4 Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4, 1.2–2 g IV infusion over 20 min). MOA: Blocks voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing intracellular calcium uptake in bronchial smooth muscle cells, resulting in smooth muscle relaxation and bronchodilation.
1.5 Fine muscle tremor (β2 stimulation in skeletal muscle), tachycardia/palpitations (reflex cardiac response and partial β1 cross-reactivity), hypokalemia (β2-mediated drive of potassium into skeletal muscle cells via Na+/K+ ATPase pump), nervousness, and headache.

Scenario 2 Inadequate Asthma Control & Controller Therapy

A 34-year-old accountant with a 5-year history of asthma visits the outpatient clinic. He reports using his salbutamol inhaler 4 to 5 times per week for daytime symptoms and wakes up at night short of breath twice a week. He is currently prescribed low-dose inhaled beclomethasone dipropionate but acknowledges irregular adherence because he feels it "doesn't provide fast relief like the blue inhaler."
2.1 Evaluate this patient's level of asthma control based on his clinical presentation and explain why relying solely on SABA as needed is inadequate and dangerous.
2.2 Explain the pharmacological mechanisms by which inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) control airway inflammation, including their influence on inflammatory mediator production and cell membrane stabilization.
2.3 How does the combination of an ICS with a Long-Acting β2-Agonist (LABA, e.g., formoterol or salmeterol) demonstrate pharmacological synergy at the cellular level?
2.4 Detail the clinical instructions and practical measures the patient must follow to prevent local adverse effects associated with inhaled corticosteroid therapy.
2.5 If this patient also suffers from exercise-induced asthma and aspirin sensitivity, which oral controller drug class could be added to his regimen, and what is its mechanism of action?

Model Answers

2.1 Classified as Uncontrolled Chronic Asthma (daytime symptoms > 2 days/week, nighttime awakenings 2 times/week). SABA-only reliance treats acute bronchospasm without addressing underlying airway inflammation, leading to progressive airway remodeling, β-receptor desensitization/down-regulation, severe exacerbations, and increased mortality.
2.2 ICS binds cytosolic glucocorticoid receptors, translocates to the nucleus, and regulates gene transcription: (a) Transactivation: ↑ synthesis of anti-inflammatory proteins (lipocortin-1, which inhibits PLA2, blocking prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis); (b) Transrepression: ↓ transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, TNF-α) and adhesion molecules; (c) Cell stabilization: inhibits mast cell degranulation and reduces eosinophil survival.
2.3 ICS up-regulates β2-adrenoceptor synthesis and prevents β2-receptor desensitization/tolerance caused by long-term LABA use. Concurrently, LABA activates PKA, which enhances glucocorticoid receptor translocation and nuclear binding, mutually boosting anti-inflammatory and bronchodilatory efficacy.
2.4 Instruct patient to use a spacer device with Metered Dose Inhaler (MDI) to reduce deposition in the pharynx, rinse the mouth thoroughly with water and gargle after each inhalation (spitting the water out), and monitor for signs of oral thrush or voice hoarseness.
2.5 Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists (LTRAs, e.g., Montelukast). MOA: Competitive antagonism of CysLT1 receptors, blocking the effects of cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4). Effective in controlling aspirin-induced and exercise-induced asthma.

Scenario 3 Narrow Therapeutic Index & Pharmacokinetic Drug Interactions

A 58-year-old male with severe chronic airway disease is maintained on oral sustained-release theophylline (300 mg twice daily). His condition has been stable with a trough serum theophylline level of 65 μmol/L. Recently, he developed a severe bacterial bronchitis and was prescribed a 7-day course of ciprofloxacin. On day 4 of antibiotic therapy, he presents to the urgent care clinic complaining of intractable nausea, vomiting, severe insomnia, persistent throbbing headache, and palpitations. His ECG shows sinus tachycardia with occasional premature ventricular contractions.
3.1 Explain the concept of narrow therapeutic index in relation to theophylline plasma concentration ranges (μmol/L) and identify the primary physiological systems affected by high serum levels.
3.2 Analyze the drug interaction between theophylline and ciprofloxacin that led to this patient's acute toxic presentation.
3.3 Name two classes of therapeutic agents or lifestyle factors that cause the exact opposite pharmacokinetic effect on theophylline (i.e., increased clearance) and explain the underlying mechanism.
3.4 Describe the multiple molecular mechanisms of action by which theophylline produces bronchodilation and anti-inflammatory effects in the respiratory tract.
3.5 Outline the immediate clinical management steps required to address theophylline toxicity in this patient.

Model Answers

3.1 Therapeutic window is narrow: 55–110 μmol/L (5–15 μg/mL). Toxicity occurs at > 110 μmol/L. Systems affected: Gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain), Central Nervous System (headache, nervousness, insomnia, severe seizures), Cardiovascular (tachycardia, palpitations, lethal tachyarrhythmias), and Renal (mild diuresis).
3.2 Ciprofloxacin is a potent inhibitor of cytochrome P450 enzymes (specifically CYP1A2) in the liver. Co-administration decreases the hepatic metabolism and clearance of theophylline, leading to marked accumulation and toxic serum concentrations.
3.3 (a) CYP450 Enzyme Inducers: Rifampicin, Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Phenobarbital; (b) Lifestyle factors: Heavy tobacco smoking and chronic ethanol consumption. Mechanism: Induction of hepatic CYP1A2 enzymes accelerates theophylline metabolism, lowering serum levels and reducing therapeutic response.
3.4 (1) Non-selective Phosphodiesterase (PDE) Inhibition: Increases intracellular cAMP and cGMP, producing smooth muscle relaxation; (2) Adenosine Receptor Antagonism: Blocks adenosine A1 receptors, preventing bronchoconstriction and central nervous system stimulation; (3) Anti-inflammatory effects: Reduces cytokine release and eosinophil infiltration.
3.5 (1) Discontinue theophylline and ciprofloxacin; (2) Monitor vital signs, ECG, and serum theophylline level; (3) Provide supportive care: IV fluids, antiemetics, and benzodiazepines for seizures; (4) In severe toxicity (arrhythmias, seizures), consider activated charcoal (if within 4 hours) and hemodialysis (for life-threatening levels).

Scenario 4 Severe Refractory Allergic & Eosinophilic Asthma (Biologic Therapies)

A 48-year-old female with severe refractory allergic asthma continues to experience frequent severe exacerbations despite adherence to high-dose ICS-LABA combination therapy plus oral montelukast. She requires 3–4 courses of oral prednisolone per year. Laboratory workup reveals: Total serum IgE = 480 IU/mL, Absolute Blood Eosinophil Count = 550 cells/μL. Her skin prick test is positive for perennial dust mite allergens. The multidisciplinary respiratory team decides to evaluate her for advanced biological therapy.
4.1 Distinguish between the immunological targets and mechanisms of action of Omalizumab versus Anti-IL-5 agents (Mepolizumab, Reslizumab, Benralizumab).
4.2 Based on this patient's lab profile (elevated IgE and eosinophils), justify which biologic therapy options are appropriate and state their route of administration.
4.3 Explain the pathophysiological role of Interleukin-5 (IL-5) in severe eosinophilic asthma and how targeting this pathway prevents exacerbations.
4.4 What critical adverse effect is specifically associated with the reduction of systemic oral corticosteroids during anti-IgE (omalizumab) therapy, and what are its key clinical features?
4.5 Identify the major safety risk and adverse effect profile associated with Anti-IL-5 therapies, including a specific infectious viral reactivation risk associated with mepolizumab.

Model Answers

4.1 Omalizumab is an Anti-IgE monoclonal antibody that binds free circulating IgE, preventing its binding to FcεRI receptors on mast cells/basophils and inhibiting IgE synthesis. Anti-IL-5 agents target the eosinophilic pathway: Mepolizumab and Reslizumab bind and neutralize IL-5 ligand, while Benralizumab binds directly to the IL-5 receptor alpha (IL-5Rα) subunit on eosinophils, inducing antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC).
4.2 Both Omalizumab (due to elevated IgE 480 IU/mL and allergic phenotype) and Anti-IL-5 agents (Mepolizumab/Benralizumab due to blood eosinophils 550 cells/μL) are appropriate options. Routes: Omalizumab (Subcutaneous), Mepolizumab (Subcutaneous), Reslizumab (Intravenous infusion), Benralizumab (Subcutaneous).
4.3 IL-5 is secreted by T2 helper cells and innate lymphoid cells. It is the principal cytokine responsible for eosinophil differentiation, maturation, recruitment, activation, and survival in the airways. Eosinophils release cytotoxic proteins (Major Basic Protein, Eosinophil Cationic Protein) causing epithelial damage. Inhibiting IL-5 decreases airway eosinophilia and prevents severe asthma exacerbations.
4.4 Churg-Strauss Syndrome (Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis). Features: Eosinophilia, vasculitic skin rash, pulmonary infiltrates, cardiac complications, and peripheral neuropathy. It often manifests when oral steroid dosages are reduced during successful omalizumab therapy.
4.5 Adverse effects include systemic hypersensitivity reactions and anaphylaxis. Specifically, Mepolizumab is associated with an increased risk of Herpes Zoster (Shingles) reactivation; varicella-zoster vaccination should be considered prior to initiating therapy.
