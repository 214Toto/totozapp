1. Describe the different forms of cerebral hemorrhage. For any one form of cerebral hemorrhage where cerebral spinal fluid examination is helpful in diagnosis, list the parameters measured and indicate the findings confirming the diagnosis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Forms of cerebral hemorrhage:

  • Intracerebral hemorrhage: Bleeding within the brain parenchyma (e.g., hypertensive, amyloid angiopathy).
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage: Bleeding into the subarachnoid space (often from ruptured aneurysm).
  • Subdural hemorrhage: Bleeding between dura and arachnoid (often traumatic).
  • Epidural hemorrhage: Bleeding between skull and dura (usually arterial, from middle meningeal artery).
  • Intraventricular hemorrhage: Bleeding into ventricles (can be extension of other hemorrhages).

CSF examination in subarachnoid hemorrhage:

  • Parameters measured: Opening pressure, colour, cell count (RBC/WBC), protein, glucose, xanthochromia, and microbiology (if infectious).
  • Findings confirming SAH:
    • Increased opening pressure (>200 mmH₂O).
    • Bloody or xanthochromic CSF (yellow after centrifugation due to oxyhemoglobin/bilirubin).
    • Elevated RBC count (no decline in tube 1 vs. tube 4).
    • Elevated protein (due to blood).
    • Normal glucose (unless coexisting infection).

2. What name is given to the condition in which there is massive splenomegaly due to malaria? Which observations are essential in making the diagnosis of this condition? Which laboratory investigations would you request to assist in confirming the diagnosis? [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Condition: Tropical Splenomegaly Syndrome (TSS) or Hyper-reactive Malarial Splenomegaly (HMS).

Essential observations for diagnosis:

  • Massive splenomegaly (often palpable below the umbilicus).
  • Presence of malarial antibodies (IgM markedly elevated).
  • Absence of other causes of splenomegaly (e.g., lymphoma, leishmaniasis).
  • Response to antimalarial therapy (splenic size reduces).

Laboratory investigations:

  • Full blood count: Anemia, thrombocytopenia, leucopenia (due to hypersplenism).
  • Malaria parasite detection: Thick/thin blood films may be negative (parasites sequestered).
  • Serum immunoglobulins: Markedly elevated IgM (polyclonal).
  • Malaria antibody titres: High levels of specific antimalarial antibodies (e.g., by ELISA or IFA).
  • Liver function tests: May show mild elevation of transaminases.
  • Abdominal ultrasound: Confirms splenomegaly and excludes other intra-abdominal pathology.
  • Bone marrow biopsy: May show reactive hyperplasia, but not specific.

3. Patient has symptoms and signs of a pituitary tumour. Why would you request laboratory and non-laboratory investigations? List the laboratory and non-laboratory investigations that would assist in confirming the diagnosis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Why request investigations? To confirm the presence of a pituitary tumour, determine its type (hormone-secreting vs non-functioning), assess its size and extent, and guide management (surgical vs medical).

Laboratory investigations:

  • Hormonal assays: Prolactin, GH, IGF-1, ACTH, cortisol, TSH, free T4, LH, FSH, testosterone/estradiol.
  • Dynamic endocrine testing: Oral glucose tolerance test (for GH suppression), ACTH stimulation test, dexamethasone suppression test.
  • Serum electrolytes: To detect diabetes insipidus (hypernatremia) or SIADH (hyponatremia).
  • Serum osmolality and urine osmolality: If diabetes insipidus suspected.

Non-laboratory investigations:

  • MRI of the pituitary gland (with gadolinium): Gold standard for pituitary tumour detection and extent.
  • Visual field examination (perimetry): To detect optic chiasm compression (bitemporal hemianopia).
  • CT scan: If MRI contraindicated.
  • Ophthalmological assessment: Fundoscopy and visual acuity.
  • Biopsy (rare): If lesion is atypical or metastatic.

4. Describe the general Pathogenesis and Laboratory diagnosis of Cryptococcus neoformans. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Pathogenesis:

  • Entry: Inhalation of basidiospores or desiccated yeast cells from environmental sources (pigeon droppings, soil).
  • Primary infection: Usually asymptomatic or mild pulmonary infection; in immunocompetent hosts, may be contained.
  • Dissemination: In immunocompromised (HIV/AIDS, transplant patients), the fungus disseminates hematogenously to CNS (meningitis), skin, bones, and viscera.
  • Virulence factors: Polysaccharide capsule (antiphagocytic), melanin production (antioxidant), and ability to grow at 37°C.
  • Immune evasion: Capsule interferes with complement activation and phagocytosis.

Laboratory diagnosis:

  • CSF examination:
    • India ink preparation: Direct visualization of encapsulated yeast (capsule appears as clear halo).
    • CSF culture: Sabouraud dextrose agar (growth in 2-5 days).
    • CSF cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) test: Latex agglutination or ELISA – highly sensitive and specific, can be done on serum too.
    • CSF cell count: Lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, low glucose.
  • Serum CrAg: Useful for screening in immunocompromised patients.
  • Blood culture: May be positive in disseminated disease.
  • Biopsy (rare): Mucicarmine stain highlights the capsule.

5. Write short notes on the features of and laboratory diagnosis of cryptococcosis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Features of cryptococcosis:

  • Clinical forms:
    • Pulmonary: Cough, fever, chest pain, may be asymptomatic in immunocompetent.
    • Meningoencephalitis: Most common presentation in immunocompromised; headache, fever, neck stiffness, altered mental status.
    • Disseminated: Skin lesions (papules, nodules), osteomyelitis, prostatitis.
  • Risk factors: HIV/AIDS (CD4 < 100), organ transplant, corticosteroids, sarcoidosis.
  • Transmission: Inhalation of aerosolized yeast from environmental sources (bird droppings, soil).

Laboratory diagnosis:

  • Direct microscopy: India ink preparation of CSF, sputum, or other fluids – shows encapsulated budding yeast.
  • Culture: Sabouraud dextrose agar (without cycloheximide) – creamy colonies at 30°C; urease positive.
  • Antigen detection: Cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) by latex agglutination or ELISA – detects capsular polysaccharide in CSF and serum; very sensitive and specific.
  • Histopathology: Mucicarmine or GMS stain on biopsy – shows encapsulated organisms.
  • Molecular: PCR for Cryptococcus DNA (not routine).
