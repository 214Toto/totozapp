B1. With the aid of a diagram, describe the stages of viral pathogenesis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Diagram: A flowchart showing: Entry → Primary replication → Viral spread (viremia) → Secondary replication in target organs → Immune response → Clearance or persistence → Disease outcome.

Stages of viral pathogenesis:

  • Entry: Virus enters the host through portals (respiratory, GI, skin, etc.)
  • Primary replication: Initial replication at the site of entry (e.g., respiratory epithelium for influenza).
  • Local spread & viremia: Virus spreads locally or via blood/lymphatics to secondary sites.
  • Secondary replication: Replication in target organs (e.g., liver for hepatitis viruses, CNS for poliovirus).
  • Immune response: Host immune system responds — innate (IFN, NK cells) then adaptive (antibodies, CTLs).
  • Viral clearance or persistence: Virus is cleared (e.g., influenza) or persists (e.g., HIV, HSV).
  • Tissue damage & disease: Direct cytopathic effects or immunopathology cause clinical disease.
  • Outcome: Recovery, chronic infection, or death.

B2. Describe briefly the condition called Polycythemia vera. List the laboratory investigations you would request to make a diagnosis. Indicate the positive findings confirming the diagnosis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Polycythemia vera (PV): A myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by increased red cell mass, often with elevated white cells and platelets. It is associated with a JAK2 V617F mutation in most cases.

Laboratory investigations:

  • Full blood count (FBC): Elevated Hb, Hct, RBC count.
  • Red cell mass: Increased (gold standard).
  • Serum erythropoietin (EPO): Low or inappropriately normal.
  • JAK2 V617F mutation analysis: Positive in >95% of PV cases.
  • Bone marrow biopsy: Hypercellular with panmyelosis.
  • Serum vitamin B12 & B12 binding capacity: Often elevated.

Positive findings confirming diagnosis:

  • Elevated Hb (>16.5 g/dL in men, >16.0 g/dL in women) or Hct (>49% in men, >48% in women).
  • Presence of JAK2 V617F mutation.
  • Low serum EPO level.
  • Hypercellular bone marrow with erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic hyperplasia.

B3. Explain the morphology, clinical features and laboratory diagnosis of Neisseria meningitidis. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Morphology: Neisseria meningitidis is a Gram-negative diplococcus (kidney-bean shaped), capsulated, and often found intracellularly in CSF. It is oxidase-positive and catalase-positive.

Clinical features:

  • Meningitis: Fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, altered mental status.
  • Meningococcemia: Petechial/purpuric rash, hypotension, DIC, adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome).
  • Septic arthritis (less common).
  • Transmission: Respiratory droplets; colonizes nasopharynx.

Laboratory diagnosis:

  • CSF analysis: Turbid CSF, elevated WBC (polymorphonuclear predominance), elevated protein, decreased glucose.
  • Gram stain of CSF: Gram-negative intracellular diplococci.
  • Culture: CSF, blood, or skin lesions. Grow on chocolate agar or blood agar (with CO₂).
  • Latex agglutination: Detects capsular antigen in CSF.
  • PCR: Rapid detection of meningococcal DNA.
  • Blood culture: Positive in bacteremic cases.

B4. A patient presents with generalized lymphadenopathy. Define what generalized lymphadenopathy is. How else does lymphadenopathy present? List the investigations that you would request to arrive at the diagnosis of a non-infectious cause of generalized lymphadenopathy. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Generalized lymphadenopathy: Enlargement of lymph nodes in two or more non-contiguous lymph node groups (e.g., cervical, axillary, and inguinal).

Other presentations of lymphadenopathy:

  • Localized: Single group of nodes (e.g., cervical only) — often due to local infection.
  • Regional: Multiple nodes in one region (e.g., axillary in breast infection).
  • Generalized: As defined above, often systemic disease.
  • Size: Can be tender (infectious) or non-tender (malignancy).
  • Consistency: Soft (infection), firm (lymphoma), or hard/irregular (metastatic).

Investigations for non-infectious cause of generalized lymphadenopathy:

  • Full blood count & peripheral smear: For leukemia/lymphoma.
  • ESR & CRP: Inflammatory markers.
  • Lymph node biopsy (excisional): Histopathology and immunohistochemistry for lymphoma.
  • Chest X-ray/CT: For mediastinal lymphadenopathy (sarcoidosis, lymphoma).
  • Serum LDH: Elevated in lymphoma.
  • Serum ACE: For sarcoidosis.
  • Autoimmune screen (ANA, RF): For connective tissue diseases.
  • HIV serology: As HIV can cause generalized lymphadenopathy.

B5. What is genetic testing? How is it done? Describe the different techniques for genetic testing. [10 Marks]

Model Answer

Genetic testing: The analysis of DNA, RNA, chromosomes, proteins, or metabolites to detect heritable or acquired mutations, used for diagnosis, prognosis, carrier screening, and pharmacogenetics.

How it is done: Specimen (blood, saliva, tissue) → DNA extraction → Amplification (PCR) → Analysis using various techniques → Interpretation.

Techniques for genetic testing:

  • PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction): Amplifies specific DNA sequences for detection of mutations, used for single-gene disorders.
  • Sanger sequencing: Determines nucleotide sequence of a DNA fragment — gold standard for single gene variants.
  • Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS): High-throughput sequencing of entire genomes or exomes for multi-gene panels or unknown mutations.
  • MLPA (Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification): Detects copy number variations (deletions/duplications).
  • Chromosomal microarray (CMA): Detects submicroscopic chromosomal gains/losses (CNVs).
  • Karyotyping: Visualizes whole chromosomes for aneuploidy and large structural rearrangements.
  • FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization): Uses fluorescent probes to detect specific chromosomal abnormalities.
  • Southern blotting: Detects large DNA rearrangements (e.g., trinucleotide repeats).
  • Mass spectrometry: For detecting inborn errors of metabolism via metabolite analysis.
