B1. What effect do glucocorticoids have on glucose control and by what mechanism do they actualise this effect? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

Glucocorticoids cause hyperglycaemia by:

  • Stimulating hepatic gluconeogenesis (increased glucose production).
  • Reducing peripheral glucose uptake (insulin resistance).
  • Promoting glycogenolysis.

They also increase plasma glucose and can precipitate or worsen diabetes mellitus.

B2. List at least four examples of tocolytics [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Atosiban (oxytocin receptor antagonist)
  • Nifedipine (calcium channel blocker)
  • Magnesium sulfate
  • Ritodrine (β2 agonist – now less used)
  • Indomethacin (NSAID – prostaglandin inhibitor)
  • Terbutaline (β2 agonist)

B3. You are a Junior Resident Medical Officer doing a tutorial on rheumatoid arthritis. What would you be required to do to initiate treatment of DMARDs? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Establish a diagnosis of RA (clinical and laboratory).
  • Assess baseline disease activity (DAS28, joint count, ESR/CRP).
  • Screen for contraindications (TB, hepatitis, blood counts, renal/liver function).
  • Choose the appropriate DMARD (usually methotrexate as first-line).
  • Educate the patient about benefits, risks, and monitoring schedule.
  • Start with low dose and titrate up, with regular monitoring (e.g., LFT, FBC).

B4. Explain the criteria of selection of DMARDs [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Disease severity and activity (high vs low).
  • Patient factors: age, comorbidities (renal, hepatic), pregnancy status.
  • Efficacy profile of the drug.
  • Safety and toxicity profile (e.g., methotrexate – hepatotoxicity, leflunomide – teratogenicity).
  • Convenience and cost (oral vs subcutaneous).
  • Previous response to therapy.

B5. A retired Army General comes ... gout symptoms worsening ... which drug would you suggest and why? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

Drug: Colchicine (or a corticosteroid like prednisolone).

Why: The patient has acute gout flare; colchicine is effective in reducing inflammation and pain if given early. However, if there are contraindications (renal impairment), NSAIDs or corticosteroids may be used. Colchicine is preferred for its rapid onset and if NSAIDs are not tolerated. Also, if the patient already has tophi or frequent flares, urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol) should be considered, but not during acute flare.

B6. What is the mechanism of action of propylthiouracil (PTU) in hyperthyroidism? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Inhibits thyroid peroxidase, blocking the oxidation of iodide and iodination of tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin.
  • Inhibits the coupling of iodotyrosines to form T4 and T3.
  • Also inhibits peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 (unique to PTU).

B7. List four clinical uses of glucocorticoids [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Autoimmune diseases (RA, SLE, inflammatory bowel disease).
  • Allergic reactions (anaphylaxis, asthma).
  • Transplant rejection prophylaxis.
  • Adrenal insufficiency (replacement therapy).
  • Inflammatory eye diseases (keratitis, uveitis).

B8. What are the adverse effects of long-term corticosteroid therapy? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Cushing's syndrome (moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity).
  • Hyperglycaemia and diabetes.
  • Osteoporosis and avascular necrosis.
  • Immunosuppression (infection risk).
  • Peptic ulcer disease.
  • Hypertension and sodium retention.

B9. Explain the mechanism of action of methotrexate in rheumatoid arthritis [2 Marks]

Model Answer

  • Inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, reducing folate levels.
  • This leads to inhibition of purine and pyrimidine synthesis.
  • Results in decreased proliferation of activated T and B lymphocytes.
  • Also increases adenosine release, which has anti-inflammatory effects.

B10. What is the role of colchicine in gout management and what are its major side effects? [2 Marks]

Model Answer

Role: Colchicine is used for acute gout flares and for prophylaxis during initiation of urate-lowering therapy.

Major side effects:

  • Gastrointestinal (diarrhoea, nausea, abdominal pain).
  • Bone marrow suppression (thrombocytopenia, leucopenia).
  • Peripheral neuropathy (with prolonged use).
  • Myotoxicity.

C1. Mwale – paracetamol overdose [5 Marks]

a. What is paracetamol toxicity? [1 mark]
b. Elaborate on the mechanism of paracetamol toxicity [2 marks]
c. How would you go about treating Mwale? [2 marks]

Model Answer

a. Paracetamol toxicity: Hepatocellular necrosis due to accumulation of toxic metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinoneimine (NAPQI) after glutathione depletion.

b. Mechanism: Normally, paracetamol is metabolised by glucuronidation and sulfation. A small fraction is oxidised by CYP450 (especially CYP2E1) to NAPQI. In overdose, glucuronidation/sulfation pathways are saturated, and excess NAPQI is produced. NAPQI is detoxified by glutathione. When glutathione is depleted, NAPQI binds covalently to hepatic proteins, causing cell necrosis.

c. Treatment:
- Activated charcoal if within 1-2 hours of ingestion.
- N-acetylcysteine (NAC) – given intravenously (or orally) to replenish glutathione and bind NAPQI.
- Supportive care – maintain hydration, monitor LFTs, INR, renal function.
- Liver transplantation in case of fulminant hepatic failure.

C2. Discuss the biguanides drugs under the following: a. Example [1 Mark] b. Mechanism of action [3 Marks]

Model Answer

a. Example: Metformin (the only biguanide in widespread use).

b. Mechanism of action:

  • Decreases hepatic glucose production (gluconeogenesis) – primary action.
  • Increases peripheral insulin sensitivity (improves glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue).
  • Reduces intestinal glucose absorption.
  • Activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which regulates energy metabolism.
  • Does not stimulate insulin release; thus low risk of hypoglycaemia.

C3. Discuss the management of gout, including acute flare treatment and long-term urate-lowering therapy [5 Marks]

Model Answer

Acute flare management:

  • NSAIDs (e.g., indomethacin, naproxen) – first-line if no contraindications.
  • Colchicine – effective if started early (within 24 hours).
  • Corticosteroids (oral, intra-articular, or IM) – for patients with contraindications to NSAIDs/colchicine.

Long-term urate-lowering therapy:

  • Target serum urate < 360 µmol/L (or < 300 if tophi).
  • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors: Allopurinol (first-line, start low and titrate) or Febuxostat.
  • Uricosurics: Probenecid (increased renal excretion) – used in under-excretors.
  • Prophylaxis with colchicine or NSAID during the first 3–6 months of urate-lowering therapy to prevent flares.
  • Lifestyle modifications: reduce purine-rich foods, alcohol, fructose; increase hydration.
